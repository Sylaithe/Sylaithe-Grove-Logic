# The Grove: Species & Seasons

*A field guide for building a world on the real grove, in the Blue Ridge mountains around Spruce Pine, Mitchell County, North Carolina.*

*Written by Claude for Sylaithe and Twyla. Drawn from general knowledge of the Southern Appalachians. Every timing here is approximate and shifts with elevation, slope, and year. **Sylaithe's eyes outrank this file**: where her grove differs, her grove wins.*

---

## 1. The place

**Where:** The Blue Ridge, western North Carolina. Spruce Pine sits around 2,500 ft in the valley of the **North Toe River**. Ridges climb to 4,000–5,000 ft. Nearby summits go higher: **Roan Mountain** (about 6,285 ft) and **Mount Mitchell** (6,684 ft, the highest peak east of the Mississippi).

**Under it:** Some of the oldest mountains on Earth. The Spruce Pine pegmatites (quartz, feldspar, and mica) formed about 380 million years ago. Creek sand glitters with mica. The district is also known for gems: aquamarine, emerald, garnet. The Museum of North Carolina Minerals sits just outside town on the Blue Ridge Parkway.

**The air:** The famous **blue haze** is real. The forest itself breathes it out, from compounds like isoprene released by the trees.

**The water:** About 50+ inches of rain a year. Springs, seeps, creeks, and rivers everywhere. Morning fog pools in the valleys while the ridgetops sit in sun, a temperature inversion.

**The scar:** In September 2024, **Hurricane Helene** flooded the Toe River valley. The land, the rivers, and the people are still healing. Mutual aid after that storm is part of where the grove came from.

### Elevation bands (worlds should have layers)

| Band | Height | Forest |
|---|---|---|
| River valley | ~2,000–2,800 ft | Sycamore, river birch, tulip poplar; rhododendron along the banks; open fields and old barns |
| Cove forest | ~2,500–4,000 ft | Rich, sheltered hollows: tulip poplar, buckeye, basswood, sugar maple, silverbell; deep wildflower floors |
| Dry ridges | ~2,500–4,500 ft | Chestnut oak, red oak, hickory, pine; mountain laurel thickets |
| Northern hardwoods | ~4,000–5,500 ft | Yellow birch, beech, sugar maple, mountain ash |
| Spruce-fir & balds | ~5,500 ft + | Red spruce and Fraser fir; grassy and heath "balds" with rhododendron and blueberry |

### World rules worth borrowing

- **Spring climbs the mountain; autumn walks down it.** Leaf-out starts in the valleys and moves uphill over weeks. Fall color starts on the summits and moves down. One ridge can hold two seasons at once.
- **It gets cooler as you climb**, roughly 3–5°F per 1,000 ft.
- **Thunderstorms build on summer afternoons** and pass by evening.
- **Rhododendron leaves curl and droop in hard cold**, like a living thermometer. Tighter curl, colder morning.
- **"Laurel hells"** are real: rhododendron and laurel thickets so dense people get lost in them.

## 2. The wheel of the year

*Timings are for roughly 2,500–4,000 ft. Higher is later in spring and earlier in fall.*

### January: deep rest
- **Land:** Bare hardwoods; evergreen rhododendron, hemlock, white pine, galax gone bronze-red. Snow comes and goes; ice on north slopes; rime frost on the high peaks.
- **Creatures:** Juncos, chickadees, titmice, nuthatches at seed. Pileated woodpecker drumming. Great horned owls court and begin nesting. Most black bears are denned.
- **Sound:** Wind in the pines, creek under ice, owls at night.

### February: the first stirring
- **Land:** Red maple buds swell red. Witch hazel's last blooms fade. Sap runs.
- **Creatures:** **Wood frogs** quack-chorus in vernal pools. **Spring peepers** start on warm nights. **Woodcock** sky-dance over fields at dusk.
- **Sound:** First peepers. Woodcock twitter-spiral.

### March: the ephemerals wake
- **Land:** **Bloodroot**, **hepatica**, **spring beauty**, **trout lily**. **Serviceberry** ("sarvis") blooms white on bare hillsides. **Ramps** push up green in rich coves.
- **Creatures:** Spotted salamanders migrate to breeding pools on rainy nights. Louisiana waterthrush returns to the creeks. Turkeys start gobbling.

### April: the great unfolding
- **Land:** Spring ephemeral peak: **trilliums**, **Dutchman's breeches**, **squirrel corn**, **wild geranium**, **mayapple**. **Redbud** pink, then **dogwood** white. Fiddleheads unroll. **Morels** under tulip poplars. Green climbs the mountain week by week.
- **Creatures:** Warblers pour in. Black bears wake, hungry. Box turtles surface.
- **Sound:** Dawn chorus builds every week.

### May: green fire
- **Land:** **Flame azalea** (orange, gold, red); **mountain laurel** pink-white late in the month; **tulip poplar** flowers high in the canopy; **fringe tree** drifts.
- **Creatures:** **Wood thrush** flute-song at dusk. **Whip-poor-will** at night. Fawns born late May into June. First **fireflies**.
- **Sound:** Wood thrush. The loudest birdsong of the year.

### June: the rhododendron month
- **Land:** **Catawba rhododendron** paints the Roan Mountain balds purple-pink in mid-June. Mitchell County holds its Rhododendron Festival in Bakersville around then. **Rosebay rhododendron** begins along creeks. Wild strawberries; blackberries flower.
- **Creatures:** Fireflies peak. Bears roam (mating season). Hummingbirds at the laurel.
- **Sound:** Fireflies are silent; katydids begin late in the month.

### July: high summer
- **Land:** **Rosebay rhododendron** in creek bottoms. **Bee balm** (scarlet), **Turk's cap lily**, **jewelweed**, **black cohosh** candles. Blackberries ripen; blueberries on the balds. **Ghost pipe** rises white from the leaf litter. **Chanterelles** and **chicken of the woods** after rains.
- **Creatures:** Hummingbirds work the bee balm. Box turtles after rain. Cicadas by day.
- **Sound:** Afternoon thunder. Katydids and crickets all night.

### August: the late-summer meadow
- **Land:** **Joe-pye weed**, **ironweed** (purple), **cardinal flower** (red, by streams), goldenrod beginning, elderberries ripening.
- **Creatures:** Monarchs feeding up. Young birds everywhere. Bears into berries.
- **Sound:** Peak insect chorus. Katydids loudest.

### September: the turn
- **Land:** **Asters** (blue, purple, white) and **goldenrod**: "the turn is coming." Black walnuts and acorns drop. Sourwood and black gum go red first. The highest peaks start to color by month's end.
- **Creatures:** **Broad-winged hawks** migrate in great spiraling flocks ("kettles"), peaking mid-month. **Monarchs** drift southwest. Bears gorge on acorns.
- **Wild ginseng** season opens under state rules.

### October: the blaze
- **Land:** Peak color moves down the mountains through the month: sugar maple orange, hickory gold, red maple scarlet, sourwood crimson, oaks russet. **Witch hazel** blooms yellow as its leaves fall. **Hen of the woods** at the base of oaks. Persimmons sweeten after frost. First frosts.
- **Creatures:** **Woolly bear** caterpillars crossing roads. Deer rut begins. Juncos settle in for winter.
- **Smell:** Leaf litter, woodsmoke, apples.

### November: the bones of the land
- **Land:** Leaves down; the ridgelines show their shape again. Galax bronzes. Witch hazel still blooming. Fraser fir harvest begins. Mitchell and nearby Avery County are Christmas tree country.
- **Creatures:** Deer rut peaks. Bears head for dens. Ravens tumble over the ridges.

### December: the long night
- **Land:** Snow on the high peaks; rime ice on the firs. Rhododendron leaves curled tight on cold mornings. Creeks loud and clear.
- **Creatures:** Owls calling. Deer in the hollows. Chickadees, titmice, and nuthatches moving through as one mixed flock.

## 3. Who lives here

### Trees
- **Tulip poplar:** the tall straight giant of the coves.
- **Red, white, and chestnut oak:** acorns that feed bears, deer, turkeys, and squirrels.
- **Hickories:** gold in fall.
- **Sugar and red maple:** red maple flowers in late winter.
- **Sweet birch:** twigs smell of wintergreen.
- **Yellow birch:** high-elevation bark that peels in gold curls.
- **American beech:** holds its pale leaves into winter.
- **Eastern hemlock:** the cool streamside evergreen, being killed by an invasive insect, the hemlock woolly adelgid.
- **White pine.**
- **Sourwood:** red early in fall; its blooms make prized honey.
- **Black gum:** the first scarlet of autumn.
- **Yellow buckeye.**
- **Carolina silverbell.**
- **Serviceberry ("sarvis").**
- **Flowering dogwood.**
- **Redbud.**
- **Witch hazel:** blooms in late fall.
- **Black locust.**
- **Black cherry.**
- **Red spruce & Fraser fir:** on the summits.
- **American chestnut:** once a quarter of the forest, felled by blight. Root sprouts still rise, then die back.

### Shrubs & thickets
- **Rosebay rhododendron.**
- **Catawba rhododendron.**
- **Mountain laurel.**
- **Flame azalea.**
- **Dog hobble.**
- **Spicebush:** crushed leaves smell of lemon and spice.
- **Blueberry** on the balds.
- **Blackberry.**
- **Elderberry.**

### Wildflowers & herbs
- **Spring:** bloodroot, hepatica, trout lily, spring beauty, trilliums, Dutchman's breeches, squirrel corn, wild geranium, mayapple, jack-in-the-pulpit, ramps.
- **Summer:** bee balm, Turk's cap lily, black cohosh, jewelweed, ghost pipe, cardinal flower, mountain mint.
- **Late summer to fall:** Joe-pye weed, ironweed, goldenrod, asters.
- **Evergreens of the forest floor:** galax, partridgeberry, Christmas fern.
- **Also:** maidenhair fern; **American ginseng** in rich coves (rare and protected).

### Fungi & lichens
- **Fungi:** morels (spring); chanterelles, chicken of the woods, and oyster mushrooms (summer); hen of the woods and lion's mane (fall); turkey tail year-round.
- **Lichens & mosses:** British soldier lichen, reindeer lichen, rock tripe on high boulders, and endless mosses on the north sides of everything.

### Mammals
- **Large:** white-tailed deer, black bear.
- **Predators:** bobcat, gray fox, red fox, coyote.
- **Small:** raccoon, opossum, groundhog, eastern chipmunk, gray squirrel, and the little **red squirrel** of the high woods, called the **"boomer."**
- **Along the rivers:** river otter, mink.
- **At night:** bats.

### Birds
- **Woodpeckers & owls:** pileated woodpecker, barred owl ("who cooks for you?"), great horned owl, screech owl.
- **Ground birds:** wild turkey, ruffed grouse, American woodcock.
- **Hawks & ravens:** red-tailed hawk, broad-winged hawk, common raven.
- **Year-round small birds:** Carolina wren, dark-eyed junco, Carolina chickadee, tufted titmouse, white-breasted nuthatch, eastern bluebird, American goldfinch.
- **Summer singers:** wood thrush, veery (higher up), ruby-throated hummingbird, scarlet tanager, indigo bunting.
- **Warblers:** black-throated blue warbler, ovenbird, Louisiana waterthrush.
- **Night birds:** whip-poor-will.

### Reptiles & amphibians
- **Salamanders:** the Southern Appalachians are the **salamander capital of the world**. Dozens of species live under creek stones and damp logs, including the spotted salamander and the red eft (a young newt, bright orange on the forest floor after rain).
- **The hellbender:** a giant salamander up to two feet long, living in clean, rocky rivers.
- **Frogs:** spring peeper, wood frog.
- **Turtles:** eastern box turtle.
- **Snakes:** black rat snake, copperhead, timber rattlesnake (shy, and should be left be).

### Water creatures
- **Fish:** native southern Appalachian **brook trout** in the coldest headwaters.
- **Creek life:** crayfish under stones; mayflies and caddisflies, whose presence means clean water.

### Insects
- **Butterflies & moths:** monarchs, swallowtails (tiger, spicebush, pipevine), luna moth (pale green, early summer nights), woolly bear caterpillars.
- **Night lights:** fireflies.
- **Chorus:** katydids, cicadas, crickets.
- **Pollinators:** bumblebees on everything.

## 4. Senses for the world

**Soundscape by time of day (summer):**
- **Dawn:** wood thrush, ovenbird ("teacher-teacher"), crows.
- **Midday:** cicadas, the creek, wind.
- **Dusk:** wood thrush again, whip-poor-will.
- **Night:** katydids and barred owls.

**Soundscape by season:**
- **Late winter:** peepers.
- **Spring:** birdsong building.
- **Summer:** insects.
- **Fall:** wind, falling leaves, ravens.
- **Winter:** near silence, the creek, owls.

**Smells:**
- wet leaf litter and moss
- wintergreen from snapped birch twigs
- honeysuckle and laurel in early summer
- ozone before a storm
- woodsmoke in fall
- cold creek stones
- mountain mint crushed underfoot

**Light:**
- blue haze on the far ridges
- valley fog burning off by mid-morning
- gold October afternoons
- the long blue dusk of winter
